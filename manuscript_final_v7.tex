\title{Orientation-Dependent Dispersion and Wave Steering in Two-Dimensional Strain-Gradient Elasticity: Directional Spectral Separation versus Complete Bragg Gaps under Material Contrast}

\author{Vipin Gupta}
\email{vipin@gurugramuniversity.ac.in}
\affiliation{Department of Mathematics, Gurugram University, Gurugram-122505, India}

\begin{abstract}
This paper investigates direction-dependent wave dispersion in two-dimensional strain-gradient elasticity when the wavelength approaches an intrinsic material length scale. The formulation combines a rotated ellipsoidal characteristic-length tensor, defined by the orientation $\theta$ and aspect ratio $\mathrm{AR}$, with local Mindlin Form-II elasticity and independent gradient micro-inertia. The Bloch--Floquet problem is discretized using a conforming 32-degree-of-freedom Bogner--Fox--Schmit element, with the Bloch phase applied consistently to displacement and derivative degrees of freedom. In the homogeneous case, orientation and aspect ratio modify the dispersion branches and produce a directional spectral separation along $\Gamma$--$X$. Its maximum value is 0.0677 at zero orientation and $\mathrm{AR}=7$. The complete-gap measure remains negative for all sampled configurations, with maximum orientation sensitivity at $\mathrm{AR}=10$. This separation is a reduced-zone property of the homogeneous medium and does not represent Bragg attenuation. On the acoustic branch, rotation of the characteristic-length tensor deflects the group velocity from the wave vector by up to $2.78^\circ$. The peak deviation remains invariant to within $10^{-5}$ degrees over the sampled orientations. In contrast, the heterogeneous epoxy--YBCO cell exhibits a positive complete Bragg gap of 43.94\% on the coarsest mesh. The gap narrows systematically with refinement and remains mesh-unconverged. Gradient micro-inertia bounds the high-wavenumber phase speed of the branch considered. The results show that orientation controls the direction of energy transport in a homogeneous strain-gradient medium, whereas complete attenuation requires material contrast.
\end{abstract}
